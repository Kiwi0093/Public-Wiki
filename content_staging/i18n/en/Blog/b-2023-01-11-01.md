# Preface

I picked up a Steam Deck—my first real toy in a long time since having kids...

As for why it took me so long to write about it, all I can say is that it's just that awesome....

It let me experience a thrill I hadn't enjoyed in a long time — the satisfaction of actually beating a game.

<!--truncate-->

# Brief Overview

Taking advantage of KOMODO's Asian distribution, I also bought a 512GB Steam Deck (even though I already have a 2230 1TB SSD on hand, I wasn't planning on modding it myself....).

I'll skip the unboxing stuff since there are already tons of unboxing articles and videos online, and just share my own thoughts...

## Primary Use

Before getting it, I honestly didn't expect to play games on it that often.... I figured I'd mess around with the hardware a bit and then it would end up collecting dust just like my PSP... Turns out I was wrong....

Coinciding with the Steam Winter Sale, I picked up a few games at a discount that were basically collection-tier picks for me:

`Octopath Traveler` and `Triangle Strategy`.. Starting with these two games, I realized my late nights were completely inseparable from the Steam Deck....

(And as a result, I later bought `Tactic Orge Reborn` while it was still pricey.)

## Quick Review

Valve actually put a lot of care into SteamOS, with many nifty built-in utilities. The few minor drawbacks are basically:

* The OS is read-only; installing new applications relies on `flatpak`.

* Valve's repository stability isn't great (though I haven't run into issues myself—probably an early-days thing).

* The jump from 3.0 --> 3.3.4 had huge changes. Personally, I think that's partly an Arch-based habit where updates bring big shifts; on the other hand, it shows Valve is actively improving their product.

* Insufficient battery life.

* The screen is only 1280x800, shy of 1080p (though on a screen this small, the difference isn't very noticeable).

* Too big and heavy (personally, it's fine in my own use).

There are quite a few pros—or rather, standout traits—as well:

* Complete UI integration. Sure, a handheld *should* get this right, but looking at other Windows-based Chinese handhelds makes it feel miles ahead.

* Ability to lock FPS to reduce power draw; whether you cap it at 40fps makes a huge difference to battery life.

* Thoughtful tweaks like Night Mode.

* Desktop Mode is available. Honestly, Desktop Mode feels a bit awkward because casual players just gaming will barely ever need it, yet when you need to set up extra things, it becomes essential (though it's definitely best used with an external keyboard and mouse).

## Handy Extra Tools

* [Steam Tinker Launch](https://github.com/sonic2kk/steamtinkerlaunch)
  
  You basically just install it through `ProtonUP-Qt`, and it's a god-tier utility. You configure it in Steam's launch options much like `Proton`. Once set up, it launches this menu before the game starts, allowing you to link a `custom program`, or check `tool -> cheat engine` to automatically launch `cheat engine` after the game starts with the process already attached—you just load the CT file directly.
  
  The only slight flaw is that when launching Cheat Engine, the touch input area doesn't quite align with the actual screen display.

* [ProtonUp-Qt](https://davidotek.github.io/protonup-qt/)
  
  An installer utility for Steam Proton and other plugins (like STL). You can install the flatpak version via Discover (or just download the AppImage). If you like, you can also install `Proton-GE` through it.

* [Proton-GE](https://github.com/GloriousEggroll/proton-ge-custom)
  
  A custom build of Proton with plenty of extra patches bundled in.
  
  [Proton GE: How Does It Differ from Proton? (Gaming) | Linux Addicts](https://www.linuxadictos.com/zh-TW/proton-ge-en-que-se-diferencia-con-proton.html)

* [Emudeck](https://www.emudeck.com/)
  
  Thanks to the Steam Deck's `excellent performance`, it's great for running emulators. Emudeck is an all-in-one installer (yes, its job is strictly unified installation; the emulators themselves are still the individual standalone emulators). Out of all these tools, this is the only one I haven't installed yet (because my SD card hasn't arrived, so I don't have the storage space).

# Conclusion

If your Steam library is full of mouse-and-keyboard-heavy PC games like `AOE` or `HOI`, then the Steam Deck isn't really for you. If you only play ultra-demanding AAA PC games, the Steam Deck isn't for you either. The Steam Deck is made for controller-friendly games with lighter loading demands, and it's ideal for people who only have fragmented pockets of time to game. As for whether you're familiar with or good at Linux, that has no serious bearing on using the device itself—at most, the better you know Linux, the more likely you are to tinker with or break SteamOS settings.

Would I recommend the Steam Deck to others??

Honestly, for the same price (or just a tiny bit more), you can get a low-end gaming laptop... and even the lowest-end gaming laptop outperforms the Steam Deck by a mile...

You might say, "But it has the dedicated SteamOS, which is awesome!" Well, if you install Manjaro without Game Mode, you can get something 87% similar to SteamOS—not to mention someone released HoloISO that can be installed on regular PC hardware (though I failed to install it in VirtualBox). So there's really no need to buy a Steam Deck just for its OS.

After narrowing it down like that, the remaining target audience seems like a bunch of people with more money than sense.... (though I'm broke, so I shouldn't belong in that category).

To me, this product is like Microsoft making the low-value Surface lineup just to teach laptop manufacturers how to design and build hardware suited for Windows 8/10 (honestly, running Windows on a Surface really was pretty pleasant—too bad I barely use Windows anymore). Valve has made real software and hardware contributions to Linux Gaming, and hopefully plenty of manufacturers will step up and compete to build even better products down the line.
