## Vibe Coding

To cut to the chase: it failed. Not only did I fail to build anything useful, I even paid for a full year of `Gemini AI Pro`. Still, there are a few takeaways I want to leave behind.

Simply put, the failure of this vibe coding experiment can't be blamed entirely on Google. Even though plenty of people online say Gemini is trash at coding, blah blah blah, objectively speaking, the main reasons for the failure came down to two things:

* Not having a clear idea of what I actually wanted to build
	Strictly speaking, it's because I lack creativity. I came up with a bunch of ideas that seemed promising, only to realize in the end that the return wasn't worth the effort, there wasn't a real need, or someone had already written a great open-source project I could just use directly.
	That killed my motivation to keep burning brain cells on it.

* Knowing too little about programming concepts—even when AI wrote a mountain of code, I couldn't read it or tell where problems might arise
	This issue was glaringly obvious. From structuring a program's framework, planning architecture, choosing a language, and defining variables, to keeping the AI from going off the rails across multiple turns and designing for future maintenance and revisions from the start—all of that was far beyond my ability to plan ahead. In other words, no matter how good AI is at writing code, if the human drafting the blueprint has an empty head, it's still useless (okay, this applies to plenty of other domains too, like governing a country, a company, or a project).

So for now, that whole effort is completely on hold. That said, strictly speaking, if I just ask AI to write simple shell scripts—things I can actually understand—it's still super handy.

### The Catch

The vision sounds great, but reality is brutally harsh—I ran into problems before even starting the work.

The biggest issue is that this home entertainment center isn't meant for just me alone. Almost all existing tools and approaches assume one big premise:

`Owner of the games = Player = Controller of everything`

The moment you have to factor in kids playing too—controlling their playtime and restricting which games they can play—you immediately land on the solution of adding a Steam child account into a Family Group so you can manage kids' playtime and game libraries via Steam's built-in features. As for emulators, since storage on a living-room device is limited, you simply put only curated games on it from the start. That sounds lovely, right? But here's the problem: if the living-room machine is powerful enough on its own and doesn't need streaming, everything is solved—it's just a standalone console. What if you need a beefier streaming host to run games that demand high-end hardware?

Long story short, I tested running games on the Steam Deck via Steam Link over my home network, and "utterly unplayable" doesn't even begin to describe it...
Meanwhile, the Moonlight/Sunshine approach has no account isolation whatsoever (since it streams the entire desktop). So if I want to include AAA titles, I'd have to dedicate a streaming host for them, and on top of that figure out how to stream the games *I* want to play, how to separate accounts and isolate sessions... Things get way too complicated, and I'd need to add more machines to pull it off.
