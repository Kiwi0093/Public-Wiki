So... this old guy had no choice but to configure and compile the kernel himself to satisfy his needs.

# Reflections

A long, long time ago, back when I used `Gentoo`, I configured and compiled a Linux kernel (how could you use `Gentoo` without doing it yourself??), but after that I never bothered doing it manually again (just like long, long ago when I was diligent enough to configure and compile my own `FreeBSD` kernel, whereas now I just use `GENERIC`).

Honestly, this hands-on attempt looked intimidating at first, though in practice it wasn't bad at all...
You just have to get used to the fact that for a WSL kernel, everything is either enabled or disabled—anything you want to use has to be built-in rather than built as a module. This is basically the same as early `FreeBSD` kernels, and conceptually different from how mainstream `Linux` kernels are used today.

And under that model, the hard part—aside from the specific features you know for sure you need (like `TC/QoS` for this time, plus `wireguard` support which I use a lot)—is remembering which modules are essential for WSL itself. That's why every guide you see recommends running:

`zcat /proc/config.gz > .config`

or

`cat /proc/config.gz | gunzip > .config`

to dump the currently running WSL kernel config as your baseline, and then simply add whatever else you need on top. That approach simplifies away all the parts that would otherwise fry your brain.

Finally... once everything is working properly, WSL is still a blast to use—especially when you're slacking off at the office on a `company Windows PC` you aren't allowed to wipe, WSL really is the salvation of us wage slaves...
